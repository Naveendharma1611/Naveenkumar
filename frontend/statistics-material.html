Module 1 — Why Statistics Matters for Data Science

Uncertainty · Evidence · Evaluation · Judgment

▼

🎯 Reasoning under uncertainty

Statistics is the discipline of drawing reliable conclusions from data that is incomplete, noisy, or sampled from a larger population. Every dataset a data scientist touches is a partial, imperfect window into some underlying process, and statistics supplies the vocabulary and the tools to say precisely how much that window can be trusted. Without it, "the average order value went up 3%" is just a sentence; with it, you can say whether that 3% is a real shift or something you'd expect to see from random variation alone.

🤖 The hidden statistics inside machine learning

Nearly every machine learning concept is statistics wearing different clothing. A train/test split is sampling. Cross-validation estimates the variance of a performance metric. A confidence interval on accuracy, an AUC score, a p-value comparing two models, a confidence that a recommender's click-through lift is real — all of it is inferential statistics. Data scientists who skip statistics tend to over-trust single numbers: one accuracy score, one A/B test result, one correlation — without ever asking how much that number would move if the data were collected again.

Without statisticsWith statistics
"Model A beat Model B by 1%""The 1% difference has a 95% CI of [-0.4%, 2.4%] — not yet distinguishable from noise"
"Sales are up this month""Sales are up 3%, outside the typical month-to-month fluctuation of ±1.5%"
"These two variables are related""r = 0.42, moderate positive association, but confounded by a third variable"

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Describe a time a single summary number (an average, a percentage, a model score) misled a decision because nobody asked how much it could vary.
  2. Why is a train/test split fundamentally a statistical sampling procedure rather than just an engineering step?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerStatistics gives data science its rigor: it is the toolkit for quantifying how much a result could change if the data were collected again, which is what separates a real finding from noise.

Module 2 — Types of Data

Categorical · Numerical · Scales · Validity

▼

🔢 Categorical vs numerical

Data splits first into categorical (qualitative, describing a quality or category) and numerical (quantitative, describing an amount). Categorical data answers "which kind?" — color, country, subscription plan. Numerical data answers "how much?" or "how many?" — age, revenue, number of clicks. This split matters immediately: a mean is meaningless for zip codes, and a bar chart is the wrong tool for a continuous variable like income.

📏 The four measurement scales

Within those two families, data has four finer scales, each permitting different operations.

ScaleExampleValid operations
NominalCountry, blood type, device OSCount, mode, equality check — no ordering
OrdinalSurvey rating (poor to excellent), education levelOrdering, median, rank-based stats — spacing between levels unknown
IntervalTemperature in °C, calendar yearAddition/subtraction, mean — no true zero, ratios meaningless
RatioHeight, income, age, countsAll arithmetic including ratios — has a true zero

⚖️ Why the scale determines which statistics are valid

Using the wrong statistic for a scale produces numbers that look legitimate but mean nothing. Averaging zip codes (nominal) or saying "40°C is twice as hot as 20°C" (interval, no true zero — not twice the thermal energy) are classic errors. Ordinal data is the trickiest in practice: a 1-5 satisfaction rating is often averaged as if it were ratio data, which is a convenient approximation but technically assumes the gap between 4 and 5 equals the gap between 1 and 2 — an assumption worth stating explicitly rather than ignoring.

Discrete vs continuous is a separate, orthogonal distinction from the four scales above — discrete numerical data takes countable values (number of orders), continuous numerical data can take any value in a range (weight, time).

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Classify these as nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio: shoe size, movie genre, Likert-scale satisfaction, revenue in dollars, exam letter grade.
  2. Explain why computing a mean on a ZIP code column is a statistical error even though the computer will happily return a number.
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerData is categorical or numerical, and within that it sits on a nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio scale — the scale determines which statistics (mean, median, ratios) are mathematically valid, so checking it before analyzing a column prevents nonsense results.

Module 3 — Measures of Central Tendency

Mean · Median · Mode · Skew

▼

📊 Mean, median, and mode

The mean (arithmetic average) sums all values and divides by the count — it uses every data point but is pulled hard by extreme values. The median is the middle value when data is sorted — it only cares about order, so it resists outliers. The mode is the most frequent value — the only central tendency measure that works for nominal data, and the only one that can be meaningfully multimodal.

MeasureBest whenWeakness
MeanRoughly symmetric data, no major outliersDistorted heavily by outliers and skew
MedianSkewed data, data with outliers (income, house prices)Ignores the actual magnitude of values
ModeCategorical data, finding the most common categoryCan be undefined, or multiple modes can tie

⚠️ When the mean lies

Household income is the textbook case: a neighborhood of 99 households earning $50,000 and one household earning $5,000,000 has a mean income near $99,500 — a number that describes no one in the neighborhood — while the median stays at $50,000, correctly describing the typical household. This is why national income and house-price statistics are almost always reported as medians. The general rule: the more skewed the distribution, the bigger the gap between mean and median, and the less trustworthy the mean is as a "typical value."

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

incomes = np.array([50_000]*99 + [5_000_000])

print("mean:  ", incomes.mean())
print("median:", np.median(incomes))
print("mode:  ", stats.mode(incomes, keepdims=True).mode[0])
Result
StatisticValue
mean99950.5
median50000.0
mode50000
As a fast skew check: if mean and median are close, the distribution is roughly symmetric. If mean is noticeably larger than median, the data is right-skewed (a few large outliers, like income or house prices); if mean is noticeably smaller, it's left-skewed.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A dataset of delivery times has mean 42 minutes and median 28 minutes. What does that gap tell you about the shape of the distribution?
  2. Why would you report median rather than mean when summarizing "typical" home price in a city?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerMean, median, and mode each answer "what's typical" differently — mean uses every value but is skewed by outliers, median is the robust middle value, and mode is the only option for categorical data — so the right choice depends on the data's scale and skew.

Module 4 — Measures of Spread

Range · Variance · Std Dev · IQR

▼

📐 Range, variance, and standard deviation

Central tendency alone hides how consistent the data is. Range (max minus min) is the simplest spread measure but is entirely determined by two points and ignores everything else. Variance measures the average squared distance of each point from the mean. Standard deviation is the square root of variance, bringing the units back to the original scale (dollars, seconds, not dollars-squared), which is why it's the number people actually report and interpret.

➗ Why variance squares the deviations

Simply averaging the raw deviations from the mean always gives zero — positive and negative deviations cancel exactly, by the definition of the mean. Squaring fixes that by making every deviation positive before averaging, and as a side effect it penalizes large deviations more than small ones, which is usually the behavior you want. The standard deviation then undoes the squaring's distortion of units by taking the square root.

MeasureFormula (sample)Units
Rangemax minus minSame as data
Variance (s²)sum of (xi − mean)² divided by (n − 1)Squared units
Standard deviation (s)square root of varianceSame as data
IQRQ3 minus Q1Same as data

🎯 The interquartile range and robustness

The interquartile range (IQR) is the span of the middle 50% of the data (Q3 − Q1, the 75th minus the 25th percentile). Like the median, it ignores extreme values, making it the standard-deviation equivalent for skewed data or data with outliers. IQR is also the basis of the common outlier rule: flag any point below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR — the rule used to draw the whiskers on a box plot.

import numpy as np

data = np.array([12, 15, 14, 10, 90, 13, 11, 16])

print("std (sample, ddof=1):", data.std(ddof=1))
q1, q3 = np.percentile(data, [25, 75])
print("IQR:", q3 - q1)
print("outlier bounds:", q1 - 1.5*(q3-q1), q3 + 1.5*(q3-q1))
n − 1 in the sample variance formula is Bessel's correction: dividing by n − 1 instead of n makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the true population variance, correcting for the fact that a sample's own mean is slightly closer to the sample's points than the true population mean would be.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Two delivery services both average 30 minutes. Service A has standard deviation 2 minutes, Service B has standard deviation 15 minutes. Which would you choose, and why does the mean alone not answer that?
  2. Why does the IQR-based outlier rule work better than a standard-deviation-based rule on strongly skewed data?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerSpread measures tell you how trustworthy the "typical value" is — variance and standard deviation use every point and are standard for roughly symmetric data, while IQR is the robust, outlier-resistant alternative used with the median on skewed data.

Module 5 — Visualizing Distributions

Histograms · Box Plots · Skewness · Shape

▼

📉 Histograms and shape

A histogram bins numerical data and plots the count per bin, turning a column of numbers into a visible shape. That shape tells you things summary statistics hide: whether the data is unimodal or has multiple peaks (suggesting mixed subpopulations), whether it's symmetric or skewed, and where most of the mass sits. Bin width matters a lot — too few bins hides structure, too many bins shows noise as if it were signal; a reasonable default is to try a few widths and see which one looks stable.

📦 Box plots and skewness at a glance

A box plot compresses a distribution into five numbers — minimum (within 1.5×IQR), Q1, median, Q3, maximum (within 1.5×IQR) — plus individual points for outliers beyond that. It is the fastest way to compare the spread and skew of several groups side by side, which a histogram grid struggles to do at a glance. Skewness quantifies asymmetry: positive (right) skew means a long tail of high values pulling the mean above the median, as with income; negative (left) skew is the mirror image, as with age at retirement in a population with a hard minimum.

Shape signalWhat it suggests
Long right tail, mean greater than medianRight-skewed — a few large outliers (income, latency, claim size)
Long left tail, mean less than medianLeft-skewed — a ceiling effect or hard upper bound nearby
Two separate peaksLikely two mixed subpopulations — consider splitting by a category
Roughly symmetric, bell-shapedCandidate for normal-distribution-based methods
Plotting mechanics — which library call draws which chart, styling, and layout — are covered in the separate Matplotlib and Seaborn study materials. This module is about reading what a shape tells you statistically, not about drawing it.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. You see a histogram of customer session length with a tall bar near zero and a long thin tail to the right. Describe the skew and name a measure of central tendency that would mislead you here.
  2. Why can a box plot comparing five product categories reveal a problem that five separate means cannot?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerHistograms and box plots exist to reveal shape, skew, and outliers that a mean and standard deviation alone can hide, which is why exploratory analysis always looks at a distribution before summarizing it with a single number.

Module 6 — Probability Fundamentals

Sample Space · Independence · Conditional · Bayes

▼

🎲 Sample space, events, and independence

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of a random process; an event is any subset of that space. Two events are independent if knowing one occurred tells you nothing about the other — P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) only holds under independence. Confusing correlated events for independent ones is a common probability mistake in practice, for example assuming two sensors fail independently when a shared power supply makes their failures correlated.

🔀 Conditional probability

Conditional probability, written P(A given B), is the probability of A given that B has already happened — it restricts the sample space to the world where B is true. It is defined as P(A and B) divided by P(B). This is the reasoning behind "the data you measure isn't the data you want" in data science: the probability a user converts given they saw an ad is a completely different number from the unconditional probability a random user converts.

🧩 Bayes' theorem, worked

Bayes' theorem flips a conditional probability around: P(A given B) equals P(B given A) times P(A), divided by P(B). It matters because the conditional probability you can measure (how a test behaves given the truth) is usually not the one you want (the truth given the test result).

Suppose a disease affects 1% of a population (the prior). A test is 99% sensitive and 95% specific, so 5% of healthy people still test positive. A random person tests positive — what's the probability they actually have the disease?

p_disease = 0.01
p_pos_given_disease = 0.99
p_pos_given_healthy = 0.05

p_pos = p_pos_given_disease * p_disease + p_pos_given_healthy * (1 - p_disease)
p_disease_given_pos = (p_pos_given_disease * p_disease) / p_pos
print(round(p_disease_given_pos, 4))
Result
QuantityValue
Probability of disease given a positive test0.1667
Even with a 99%-sensitive, 95%-specific test, a positive result only means a 16.7% chance of actually having the disease — because the disease is rare, false positives from the large healthy population outnumber true positives. This is one of the most common probability mistakes people make when reading medical or fraud-detection test results: reading sensitivity as if it already answers "given positive, what's the chance it's real?"

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Explain in plain language why a highly accurate test can still produce mostly false positives when the condition being tested for is rare.
  2. Give a data science example (fraud detection, spam filtering, churn) where mistaking one conditional direction for the other would lead to a wrong conclusion.
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerProbability gives data science its formal language for uncertainty, and Bayes' theorem is the tool for inverting a conditional probability — most famously showing that a highly accurate test for a rare condition still produces mostly false positives among those who test positive.

Module 7 — Common Probability Distributions

Normal · Binomial · Poisson · Uniform

▼

🌐 Matching a distribution to a process

A probability distribution is a model of what outcomes to expect and how likely each one is. Picking the right one is picking the right assumptions about the process generating your data — get it wrong and every downstream calculation (a confidence interval, a p-value, a control chart) inherits the error.

DistributionModelsKey parametersExample
NormalContinuous data clustering around a center, symmetricmean, standard deviationHeights, measurement error, sums of many effects
BinomialCount of successes in n independent yes/no trialsn trials, success probability pNumber of conversions out of 1,000 visitors
PoissonCount of rare events in a fixed time or space windowrate lambdaWebsite errors per hour, customer arrivals per minute
UniformEvery outcome in a range equally likelyminimum, maximumA fair die, a random number generator's output

🪙 Binomial and Poisson in practice

The binomial distribution answers "out of n independent trials each with success probability p, how many successes?" — the natural model for conversion counts, A/B test successes, or defect counts out of a fixed batch. The Poisson distribution answers "how many events occur in a fixed window, given they happen at some average rate and independently of each other?" — it is the binomial's limit when n is very large and p is very small, which is why it models rare, independent events like server crashes per day or calls arriving at a support line per minute.

from scipy import stats

# Binomial: probability of exactly 60 conversions out of 1000 visits at p=0.05
print(stats.binom.pmf(60, n=1000, p=0.05))

# Poisson: probability of exactly 3 errors in an hour if average rate is 2/hour
print(stats.poisson.pmf(3, mu=2))

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Website checkout failures occur rarely and seemingly at random throughout the day. Which distribution would you reach for first to model the number of failures per hour, and why?
  2. You run an A/B test with 500 visitors per arm and track conversions. Which distribution naturally models the conversion count in each arm?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerDifferent real-world processes produce different distribution shapes — normal for symmetric continuous measurements, binomial for counts of successes in fixed trials, Poisson for rare independent events in a window — and choosing the matching distribution is what makes downstream probability calculations valid.

Module 8 — The Normal Distribution in Depth

Empirical Rule · Z-scores · Standardization

▼

🔔 The empirical rule (68-95-99.7)

The normal distribution is fully described by just two numbers: its mean (center) and standard deviation (spread). The empirical rule says that for any normal distribution, about 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% fall within 2, and about 99.7% fall within 3. This rule is what makes "two standard deviations from the mean" an intuitive shorthand for "unusually far out" across an enormous range of applications, from quality control to anomaly detection.

📍 Z-scores and standardization

A z-score converts any value into "how many standard deviations from the mean," via z = (x minus mean) divided by standard deviation. Standardizing a value this way lets you compare numbers that live on completely different scales — a test score and a salary — on one common footing, and it is also the mechanism behind feature scaling before training distance-based ML models like k-NN or k-means.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

mu, sigma = 70, 10
x = 85

z = (x - mu) / sigma
print("z-score:", z)
print("percentile:", stats.norm.cdf(z) * 100)
Result
QuantityValue
z-score1.5
percentile93.32

📐 Why the normal distribution shows up everywhere

The normal distribution appears so often because of the Central Limit Theorem (covered fully in Module 10): whenever a measured quantity is the sum or average of many small, independent effects, that quantity tends toward a normal shape regardless of the shape of the individual effects. Human height, measurement error, and test scores are all the net result of many small contributing factors, which is why they cluster into a bell curve.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A score of 85 comes from a distribution with mean 70 and standard deviation 7.5. Compute its z-score and say roughly what percentile that corresponds to using the empirical rule.
  2. Why is standardizing features to z-scores often a required preprocessing step before k-nearest-neighbors or k-means clustering, but not before a decision tree?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerThe normal distribution is defined entirely by its mean and standard deviation, the empirical rule gives quick intuition for how far out a value sits (68/95/99.7% within 1/2/3 standard deviations), and z-scores standardize any value onto that same scale for comparison.

Module 9 — Sampling & Sampling Distributions

Population · Sample · Sampling Error · Sample Size

▼

🧪 Population vs sample

The population is the entire group you want to know something about; a sample is the subset you actually observe and measure. Data science is almost always working with a sample pretending to speak for a population — all of a company's future customers, not just the ones who've converted so far. The parameters of a population (true mean, true proportion) are usually unknowable; the statistics of a sample (sample mean, sample proportion) are estimates of them, and estimates carry error.

🎯 Sampling error and why sample size matters

Sampling error is the natural, unavoidable gap between a sample statistic and the true population parameter, caused purely by which particular individuals ended up in the sample. It is not a mistake — it exists even with perfect random sampling and perfect measurement. What shrinks sampling error is sample size: the standard error of the sample mean shrinks proportionally to 1 over the square root of n, so quadrupling your sample size only halves your typical error. This square-root relationship is why doubling a dataset rarely doubles precision, and why very high precision requires disproportionately more data.

Sampling methodHow it worksRisk
Simple random samplingEvery individual has equal chance of selectionNeeds a full list of the population to draw from
Stratified samplingSplit population into subgroups, sample within eachRequires knowing the right subgroups in advance
Convenience samplingUse whoever is easiest to reach (e.g. app users who opted in)High risk of selection bias — not representative
Cluster samplingRandomly select whole groups (e.g. stores), sample everyone in themIndividuals within a cluster are correlated, reducing effective sample size
A biased sampling method cannot be fixed by collecting more data — a larger convenience sample is still a convenience sample. More data reduces sampling error (random noise); it does nothing for sampling bias (a systematic skew in who gets included).

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A mobile app surveys only users who open the app at least once a week to estimate "overall user satisfaction." What kind of sampling problem is this, and would collecting 10x more responses fix it?
  2. Why does cutting the standard error in half require 4x the sample size rather than 2x?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerA sample is only useful to the extent it represents the population it's drawn from — random sampling controls sampling error, which shrinks with the square root of sample size, but no amount of additional data fixes a biased sampling method.

Module 10 — The Central Limit Theorem

Sampling Distribution · Normality · Inference Foundation

▼

♾️ What the CLT actually guarantees

The Central Limit Theorem states that the distribution of the sample mean (not the raw data) approaches a normal distribution as sample size grows, regardless of the shape of the original population distribution — as long as the population has a finite variance and the samples are independent. This is a statement about the sampling distribution of a statistic, not about the data itself: you can sample from a wildly skewed distribution (like income or wait times) and the distribution of sample means across many repeated samples will still look normal once n is large enough, typically n of 30 or more as a rough rule of thumb.

🏗️ Why it's the foundation of inference

Nearly every classical inference method — confidence intervals, t-tests, z-tests — relies on the sampling distribution of a statistic being approximately normal so that well-understood normal-distribution math can be applied to compute p-values and interval widths. The CLT is what licenses that assumption even when the underlying data clearly isn't normal, which is why it is arguably the single most load-bearing theorem in applied statistics: without it, hypothesis testing would need a different, distribution-specific toolkit for every kind of data.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
population = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=100_000)   # heavily right-skewed

sample_means = [rng.choice(population, size=40, replace=False).mean() for _ in range(2000)]

print("population mean vs median:", np.mean(population), np.median(population))
print("sample-mean distribution mean:", np.mean(sample_means))
print("sample-mean distribution std (standard error):", np.std(sample_means))
Even though the exponential population above is strongly right-skewed, the 2,000 sample means computed from it cluster into a roughly bell-shaped distribution — that's the CLT in action, and it's why plotting a histogram of sample_means makes it visible.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. You sample 40 customers' spend from a population you know is heavily right-skewed. Explain why the distribution of the sample mean across many such samples would still look approximately normal.
  2. Why does the "n of 30 or more" rule of thumb need a larger n when the underlying population is more heavily skewed?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerThe Central Limit Theorem guarantees that the sampling distribution of the mean approaches normal as sample size grows, regardless of the population's original shape, which is the reason normal-distribution-based methods like confidence intervals and t-tests work even on non-normal data.

Module 11 — Confidence Intervals

Interpretation · Computation · Misreadings

▼

📏 What "95% confidence" actually means

A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter, built from sample data. The correct interpretation of "95% confidence interval" is about the procedure: if you repeated the sampling and interval-construction process many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true population parameter. It is not a statement that there's a 95% probability the true parameter lies in this one specific interval you calculated — the true parameter is a fixed (if unknown) number, not a random variable, so once the interval is computed it either does or doesn't contain the truth.

🧮 Computing and interpreting one

For a sample mean with estimated standard deviation, a confidence interval is the sample mean plus or minus a critical value times the standard error, where standard error equals sample standard deviation divided by the square root of n. The critical value comes from the normal or t-distribution depending on sample size and whether the population standard deviation is known.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

sample = np.array([22, 25, 19, 27, 24, 23, 26, 21, 28, 20])
n = len(sample)
mean = sample.mean()
se = sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)

ci = stats.t.interval(confidence=0.95, df=n-1, loc=mean, scale=se)
print("mean:", mean, "95% CI:", ci)
Result
QuantityValue
sample mean23.5
95% CI(21.27, 25.73)

⚠️ Common misinterpretations

Three misreadings come up constantly: treating "95% confidence" as "95% probability the true value is in this interval" (wrong, see above); assuming a narrower interval always means a better estimate, when it might just mean a smaller or less variable sample happened to be drawn; and assuming two groups' confidence intervals overlapping means their difference is not significant — overlapping CIs can still correspond to a statistically significant difference, so overlap is a rough visual heuristic, not a formal test.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Rewrite "there's a 95% chance the true mean is between 21.3 and 25.7" into a statement that correctly describes what a 95% confidence interval actually guarantees.
  2. Why can a confidence interval for group A and a confidence interval for group B overlap, yet a direct two-sample test still find the difference between A and B statistically significant?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerA 95% confidence interval means that if you repeated the sampling procedure many times, 95% of the intervals constructed that way would contain the true parameter — it describes the reliability of the method, not a probability about this one fixed interval.

Module 12 — Hypothesis Testing Fundamentals

Null · Alternative · Test Statistic · P-value

▼

🧭 Null vs alternative hypothesis

Every hypothesis test starts with two competing claims. The null hypothesis is the default, "nothing interesting is happening" claim — no difference, no effect, no relationship. The alternative hypothesis is what you'd conclude if the evidence against the null is strong enough. A test never "proves" the null true — it only ever fails to find enough evidence to reject it, which is a subtly but importantly weaker claim.

📐 The test statistic and the p-value

A test statistic compresses the sample data into a single number measuring how far the observed result is from what the null predicts, scaled by how much variation you'd expect by chance. The p-value is the probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you got, if the null hypothesis were actually true. A small p-value means the observed data would be a rare, surprising outcome under the null, which is evidence against it.

The p-value is NOT the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it is NOT the probability that the result happened by chance. It is a conditional probability computed assuming the null is true: the probability of data this extreme or more, given the null. Confusing it with the probability the null is true, given the data, is one of the most common statistics errors in applied work, including in published research.
Correct reading of p = 0.03Incorrect reading of p = 0.03
"If the null were true, data this extreme would occur about 3% of the time""There's a 3% chance the null is true"
"The evidence against the null is fairly strong""There's a 97% chance the effect is real"
"Reject the null at the common 0.05 threshold""The effect size must be large or important"

Module 13 — Type I & Type II Errors

False Positive · False Negative · Power · Alpha

▼

⚠️ False positive vs false negative

A Type I error is a false positive: rejecting the null when it's actually true — concluding there's an effect when there isn't one. A Type II error is a false negative: failing to reject the null when it's actually false — missing a real effect. The two errors trade off against each other: making a test stricter to avoid false positives makes it more likely to miss real effects, and vice versa.

DecisionNull is actually trueNull is actually false
Test rejects the nullType I error (false positive), probability alphaCorrect decision (true positive)
Test fails to reject the nullCorrect decision (true negative)Type II error (false negative), probability beta

🎚️ Significance level and power

Significance level (alpha), almost always set at 0.05 by convention, is the Type I error rate you're willing to accept — the probability of a false positive if the null is really true. Power (1 minus beta) is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect when one exists; it rises with larger sample size, larger true effect size, and lower measurement noise. Low-power studies are dangerous specifically because when they do find "significant" results, those results are disproportionately likely to be false positives or wildly overestimated effect sizes — a widely underappreciated point in applied analytics.

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

# Quick power estimate for a two-sample test via simulation
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_per_group, p_control, p_treatment, alpha = 400, 0.10, 0.13, 0.05
sig_count = 0
trials = 2000
for _ in range(trials):
    control = rng.binomial(1, p_control, n_per_group)
    treatment = rng.binomial(1, p_treatment, n_per_group)
    _, p = stats.ttest_ind(control, treatment)
    if p < alpha:
        sig_count += 1
print("estimated power:", sig_count / trials)

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A medical screening test is tuned to minimize false negatives (Type II errors) even at the cost of more false positives. Why might that trade-off be the right call for a screening test specifically?
  2. An A/B test runs with only 50 users per arm and finds no significant difference. Is that strong evidence of "no effect," or could it be a power problem? Explain.
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerType I is a false positive (rejecting a true null), Type II is a false negative (missing a real effect), significance level alpha controls how often you accept Type I risk, and power is your ability to avoid Type II — the two always trade off against each other for a fixed sample size.

Module 14 — t-Tests

One-Sample · Two-Sample · Paired · Assumptions

▼

🧮 One-sample, two-sample, and paired t-tests

A t-test compares means when the population standard deviation is unknown and must be estimated from the sample — the standard situation in practice. A one-sample t-test compares a sample mean to a known or hypothesized value (is average delivery time different from the promised 30 minutes?). An independent two-sample t-test compares the means of two separate, unrelated groups (control vs treatment). A paired t-test compares two measurements taken on the same subjects (before vs after), which removes individual-to-individual variation and is usually far more powerful than treating the two measurements as independent groups.

✅ Assumptions behind each

t-tests assume the underlying data (or more precisely the sampling distribution of the mean) is approximately normal — a mild assumption for large samples thanks to the CLT, but one worth checking for small samples — and that observations are independent. The two-sample t-test additionally has a variant, Welch's t-test, that does not assume equal variances between the two groups, which is the safer default since equal variances is a strong and often false assumption.

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

control = np.array([5.1, 4.9, 5.3, 5.0, 4.8, 5.2, 5.1, 4.7])
treatment = np.array([5.6, 5.4, 5.9, 5.3, 5.7, 5.5, 5.8, 5.2])

t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(control, treatment, equal_var=False)   # Welch's t-test
print("t-statistic:", round(t_stat, 3), "p-value:", round(p_val, 4))

  2. Why is Welch's t-test (unequal variances) usually a safer default than the classic Student's t-test (equal variances)?

Module 15 — Chi-Square & ANOVA

Association · Multiple Groups · F-statistic

▼

🔬 Chi-square test for categorical association

A chi-square test of independence checks whether two categorical variables are associated — for example, whether device type (mobile or desktop) is associated with purchase outcome (bought or didn't). It compares observed counts in a contingency table against the counts you'd expect if the variables were truly independent, and produces a large test statistic (and small p-value) when the observed and expected counts diverge a lot.

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency
import numpy as np

table = np.array([
    [180, 220],   # mobile: bought, didn't buy
    [140, 260],   # desktop: bought, didn't buy
])
chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(table)
print("chi2:", round(chi2, 2), "p-value:", round(p, 4))

🧪 ANOVA for comparing more than two means

Running separate t-tests on every pair of groups inflates the overall false-positive rate — with 5 groups that's 10 pairwise tests, each carrying a 5% false-positive risk, compounding to a much higher chance of at least one spurious "significant" result. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) solves this by testing all groups at once: it asks whether the variance between group means is large relative to the variance within groups, producing one F-statistic and one p-value for "are any of these group means different?" A significant ANOVA result says at least one group differs, not which one — that requires a follow-up post-hoc test, such as Tukey's HSD.

TestVariable typesQuestion answered
Chi-squareTwo categorical variablesAre these two categorical variables associated?
t-testOne categorical (2 groups) plus one numericalDo these two groups' means differ?
ANOVAOne categorical (3+ groups) plus one numericalDo any of these 3+ groups' means differ?
Both chi-square and ANOVA only tell you that a relationship or difference likely exists, not how strong or practically important it is — always pair the test with an effect-size measure (Cramer's V for chi-square, eta-squared for ANOVA) before deciding the result matters.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. You want to know whether customer satisfaction (1-5 rating) differs across four store regions. Which test fits, and why would running six separate pairwise t-tests instead be a statistical mistake?
  2. A chi-square test finds a significant association between browser type and churn, with p = 0.001. What does this result NOT tell you that you'd still need to investigate?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerChi-square tests whether two categorical variables are associated by comparing observed versus expected counts, while ANOVA tests whether three or more group means differ at once using a single F-statistic — both exist specifically to avoid the inflated false-positive rate of running many separate pairwise tests.

Module 16 — Correlation

Pearson · Spearman · Confounding

▼

🔗 Pearson vs Spearman

Pearson's r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two numerical variables, ranging from -1 (perfect negative linear) through 0 (no linear relationship) to +1 (perfect positive linear). Spearman's rank correlation instead measures the strength of a monotonic relationship (consistently increasing or decreasing, not necessarily in a straight line) by correlating the ranks of the values rather than the values themselves — which makes it robust to outliers and able to capture non-linear-but-monotonic patterns Pearson would understate.

r valueTypical interpretation
0.0 to 0.1Negligible
0.1 to 0.3Weak
0.3 to 0.5Moderate
0.5 to 0.7Strong
0.7 to 1.0Very strong

🚫 Why correlation isn't causation

A correlation says two variables move together; it says nothing about why. The classic confounding example: ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly positively correlated across months of the year — not because ice cream causes drowning, but because a third variable, hot weather, independently drives both more ice cream purchases and more swimming (and thus more drowning risk). Treating a correlation as causal without ruling out confounders, reverse causation, or coincidence is one of the most common and consequential analytical errors.

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7])
y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 5, 7, 10])

pearson_r, _ = stats.pearsonr(x, y)
spearman_r, _ = stats.spearmanr(x, y)
print("Pearson r:", round(pearson_r, 3), " Spearman r:", round(spearman_r, 3))

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate strongly by month. Name the confounding variable and explain how it independently drives both.
  2. Give a data science example where Spearman's correlation would reveal a relationship that Pearson's correlation would mostly miss.
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerPearson's r captures linear association and Spearman's captures monotonic association via ranks, but neither one implies causation — a strong correlation can always be explained by a confounding variable, reverse causation, or coincidence, which is why correlation is evidence to investigate, not a conclusion.

Module 17 — Simple Linear Regression as a Statistical Model

Coefficients · R² · Residuals · Assumptions

▼

📈 Coefficients as estimated relationships

Simple linear regression fits a line through the data, predicting y from an intercept plus a slope times x. Statistically, the slope is not just a number from an optimization routine — it's a parameter estimate with its own standard error, confidence interval, and p-value (testing the null hypothesis that x has no linear relationship with y), exactly like any other statistic covered in this material.

📊 R² and residuals

R² (coefficient of determination) is the proportion of variance in y explained by the model, ranging from 0 (model explains nothing beyond the mean) to 1 (model explains all variance). Residuals (actual minus predicted) are where the statistical diagnostics live: plotting residuals against fitted values should show no pattern — a funnel shape signals non-constant variance (heteroscedasticity), a curve signals a missing non-linear term, and both violate the model's assumptions even if R² looks reasonable.

AssumptionWhat it meansConsequence if violated
LinearityTrue relationship between x and y is a straight lineBiased, misleading coefficient estimates
IndependenceResiduals aren't correlated with each otherStandard errors (and p-values) are wrong
HomoscedasticityResidual variance is constant across xStandard errors are wrong, CIs too narrow or wide
Normality of residualsResiduals are approximately normalMainly affects small-sample inference, not predictions

🔀 The statistical framing vs the ML framing

Machine learning courses often present linear regression purely as a prediction algorithm minimizing squared error. The statistical framing adds a layer ML tends to skip: is the slope distinguishable from zero, how uncertain is that estimate, and are the assumptions underneath the model even satisfied? A model can have excellent predictive R² on a test set while still having a coefficient whose confidence interval straddles zero on a smaller slice of the data — the two framings answer different questions and a working data scientist needs both.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8])
y = np.array([2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 7.8, 10.1, 11.9, 14.3, 15.8])

slope, intercept, r, p, se = stats.linregress(x, y)
print(f"slope={slope:.3f}  intercept={intercept:.3f}  R2={r**2:.3f}  p={p:.5f}")

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A regression's R² is 0.85 but a residual-vs-fitted plot shows a clear funnel shape. Explain why the high R² doesn't mean the model's confidence intervals and p-values can be trusted.
  2. What does it mean, statistically, for a slope coefficient's 95% confidence interval to include zero, and what should you conclude about x's relationship to y?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerStatistically, regression coefficients are estimates with their own standard errors and p-values, not just optimized numbers — so a working regression analysis checks whether the slope is distinguishable from zero and whether residual plots validate linearity, independence, and constant variance, not just whether R² looks high.

Module 18 — Bias, Confounding & Common Pitfalls

Selection Bias · Survivorship · Simpson's Paradox

▼

🕳️ Selection bias and survivorship bias

Selection bias occurs when the way data is collected systematically favors certain outcomes, so the sample no longer represents the population you care about — surveying only app power-users about "overall satisfaction" is selection bias. Survivorship bias is a specific, famous flavor of it: analyzing only the entities that "survived" some filtering process while the failures are invisible. The canonical example is WWII aircraft armor: engineers wanted to reinforce the parts of returning planes with the most bullet holes, until statistician Abraham Wald pointed out those planes survived despite those hits — the armor belonged on the spots with no holes, because planes hit there never made it back to be studied.

🔀 Simpson's paradox, worked

Simpson's paradox is when a trend appears in several groups of data but reverses or disappears when the groups are combined — caused by a lurking variable unevenly distributed across the groups.

HospitalPatients treatedSurvival, mild casesSurvival, severe casesOverall survival rate
Hospital A1000 mild, 100 severe95%60%92.3%
Hospital B200 mild, 800 severe98%70%75.6%

Hospital A looks worse within mild cases (95% vs 98%) and within severe cases (60% vs 70%) — it loses on both subgroups — yet Hospital A's overall survival rate (92.3%) looks far better than Hospital B's (75.6%). The paradox resolves once you notice Hospital B treats far more severe cases, which pulls its overall rate down even though it's actually better within each severity level. The lesson: an aggregated statistic can reverse the truth found in every subgroup when group sizes differ systematically, so always check whether a hidden grouping variable — here, case severity — is unevenly distributed before trusting an overall number.

🔗 Confounding variables

A confounding variable influences both the supposed cause and the supposed effect, creating an association between them that has nothing to do with one causing the other — as with ice cream and drowning in Module 16, where hot weather confounds the relationship. Spotting confounders requires domain knowledge statistics alone can't supply; the statistical contribution is controlling for them, via stratification, regression adjustment, or randomized experiments that break the confounder's link to the "cause" variable.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A company finds that employees who use a new productivity tool have higher performance reviews. List two confounders that could fully explain this without the tool causing anything.
  2. Construct your own two-group example (any domain) where Simpson's paradox could occur, and identify what the lurking grouping variable would be.
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerSelection bias, survivorship bias, and Simpson's paradox are all versions of the same risk — that the group you're looking at, or the way it's split, isn't representative of the truth you're trying to measure — and the fix is always the same discipline: ask who or what is missing or unevenly distributed before trusting an aggregate number.

Module 19 — A/B Testing in Practice

Design · Power · Peeking · Multiple Comparisons

▼

🧫 Designing a test

A good A/B test starts with one clearly defined metric and a minimum detectable effect worth caring about before any data is collected — deciding what counts as a win after seeing the results invites motivated reasoning. Users (or sessions, depending on the unit of randomization) are randomly assigned to control and treatment, which is what allows any observed difference to be attributed to the treatment rather than to pre-existing differences between the groups — randomization does the same job a confounder-control strategy does in observational data, except by construction rather than by adjustment.

📐 Sample size and power considerations

Before running a test, a power analysis answers "how many users do I need to reliably detect the effect size I care about?" It depends on four things: the significance level, the desired power, the baseline rate, and the minimum effect size worth detecting. Smaller effects, or noisier metrics, require disproportionately larger samples — this is why tiny conversion-rate lifts (going from 5.0% to 5.2%) often need tens of thousands of users per arm to detect reliably, while a large, obvious effect can be confirmed with a much smaller test.

from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize

effect_size = proportion_effectsize(0.052, 0.050)   # baseline 5.0% -> 5.2%
analysis = NormalIndPower()
n_per_group = analysis.solve_power(effect_size, power=0.8, alpha=0.05, ratio=1.0)
print("required users per arm:", round(n_per_group))

⚠️ Common mistakes

Peeking early and stopping on significance is the most common A/B testing error: checking results daily and stopping the moment p drops below 0.05 inflates the real false-positive rate far above the nominal 5%, because you're effectively running many tests and taking the best-looking one. The fix is to commit to a sample size (or use a sequential testing method designed for repeated looks) before starting, and not stop early just because a result looks good. Running many simultaneous metrics or variants without correcting for multiple comparisons has the same root cause — testing 20 metrics at a 0.05 threshold each means roughly one will look "significant" purely by chance, so a correction (like Bonferroni) or a single pre-registered primary metric is needed.

MistakeWhy it's a problemFix
Peeking and stopping earlyInflates real false-positive rate well above 5%Fix sample size in advance, or use sequential testing methods

Ignoring network or interference effectsTreatment can leak into control (e.g. social features)Randomize at a level that avoids leakage (e.g. by region, not by user)
Stopping because of a dip in an unrelated metricConfuses noise in a secondary metric with a real signalDefine the decision rule and guardrail metrics before launch

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A product manager wants to check the dashboard every morning and ship the variant the moment it crosses the 0.05 threshold. Explain concretely why this inflates the real false-positive rate.
  2. You're testing 15 different headline variants against a control on the same metric. Why does finding one "significant" winner at p = 0.04 deserve extra scrutiny?
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerA well-run A/B test fixes its sample size and primary metric before launch using a power analysis, randomizes to rule out confounding, and never stops early on a good-looking p-value — because repeated peeking and testing many metrics both inflate the real false-positive rate far above the nominal 5%.

Module 20 — Bayesian vs Frequentist Thinking

Priors · Posterior · Belief vs Frequency

▼

⚖️ The core philosophical difference

Frequentist statistics treats the population parameter as a fixed, unknown constant and probability as a long-run frequency across repeated sampling — a frequentist p-value or confidence interval is a statement about the behavior of a procedure across hypothetical repetitions, never about the probability of a specific hypothesis. Bayesian statistics treats probability as a degree of belief, allowing direct probability statements about parameters and hypotheses themselves — a Bayesian can say "there is an 80% probability the true conversion lift is above 2%," a sentence a frequentist framework structurally cannot produce.

🔄 Prior, likelihood, posterior

Bayesian inference updates a prior belief about a parameter (what you believed before seeing this data, possibly from past experience or expert judgment) using the likelihood of the observed data under different parameter values, producing a posterior — an updated belief that combines both. Formally this is just Bayes' theorem from Module 6 applied to parameters instead of events: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. As more data arrives, the likelihood dominates and the posterior becomes less sensitive to the choice of prior — but with small data, the prior genuinely steers the conclusion, which is exactly the point when there is real, legitimate prior knowledge to incorporate.

AspectFrequentistBayesian
ParameterFixed, unknown constantTreated as a random variable with a distribution
Probability statementAbout the long-run behavior of a procedureDirectly about the parameter or hypothesis
Prior informationNot formally incorporatedExplicitly incorporated via a prior distribution
OutputPoint estimate, p-value, confidence intervalFull posterior distribution, credible interval

💡 When Bayesian reasoning is practically useful

Bayesian methods pay off most clearly with small data where a sensible prior genuinely helps (e.g. a new product with few users but strong analogous data from similar past launches), with sequential decision-making where beliefs need continuous updating as data trickles in (many modern A/B testing platforms use Bayesian methods for exactly this reason — they allow more natural "stop when probability of a winner is high enough" rules), and whenever stakeholders want a direct, intuitive probability statement about a business outcome rather than a p-value's awkward double negative. In large-data, well-specified, one-shot analysis settings, Bayesian and frequentist methods often converge to nearly identical practical answers — the choice matters most exactly where data is scarce or sequential.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. A stakeholder asks "what's the probability our new feature actually increases revenue?" Explain why a frequentist p-value cannot directly answer that question, but a Bayesian posterior can.
  2. Describe a situation at a startup with very little data where incorporating a sensible prior would meaningfully improve a decision over a purely frequentist analysis.
🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerFrequentist statistics treats the parameter as fixed and makes probability statements about a repeated procedure, while Bayesian statistics treats the parameter as uncertain and updates a prior into a posterior using the data — Bayesian reasoning is most valuable with small or sequentially arriving data where a sensible prior and direct probability statements about outcomes are worth the extra modeling complexity.

Module 21 — Interview Questions

Concepts · Confident Answers · Trade-offs

▼

Q: What's the difference between mean, median, and mode, and when would each mislead you?

The mean is the arithmetic average and uses every value, the median is the middle value when sorted, and the mode is the most frequent value. The mean gets pulled by outliers and skew — a single huge income can drag a neighborhood's average far above what any household actually earns — while the median resists that because it only cares about order. I'd reach for the median whenever the data is skewed or has outliers, like income, house prices, or latency, and reserve the mean for roughly symmetric distributions.

Q: What's the difference between a population and a sample, and why does it matter?

The population is the entire group you want to understand, and the sample is the subset you actually observe. It matters because every statistic computed on a sample — a mean, a proportion, a correlation — is an estimate of the true population parameter, not the parameter itself, so it carries sampling error. The size and representativeness of the sample determine how much you can trust that estimate; a biased sample, no matter how large, still misrepresents the population.

Q: How do you interpret a p-value, and what's the most common misinterpretation?

A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as what you got, assuming the null hypothesis is true. The most common misinterpretation is treating it as the probability the null hypothesis itself is true, or as the probability the result happened "by chance" — neither is correct, because the p-value is computed under the assumption the null is already true. I always pair a p-value with an effect size, because statistical significance doesn't imply practical importance.

Q: What are Type I and Type II errors, and how do you decide which to minimize?

A Type I error is a false positive — rejecting a true null hypothesis — and a Type II error is a false negative — failing to reject a false null. Which one to minimize depends on the cost of each mistake in context: a cancer screening test is tuned toward fewer false negatives because missing a real case is far costlier than a follow-up scan, while a spam filter might be tuned toward fewer false positives because losing a real email is worse than letting some spam through. You can't minimize both simultaneously for a fixed sample size — reducing one raises the other.

Q: What does a 95% confidence interval actually mean?

It means that if you repeated the sampling and interval-construction procedure many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true population parameter. It does not mean there's a 95% probability the true value falls in this one specific interval — the true value is fixed, and the interval either contains it or doesn't. I think of the confidence level as describing the reliability of the method, not a probability about this particular result.

Q: Why is the Central Limit Theorem important in practice?

The CLT guarantees that the sampling distribution of the mean becomes approximately normal as sample size grows, no matter what shape the underlying population has. That's what licenses using normal-distribution-based tools — confidence intervals, t-tests, z-tests — even on data that is itself skewed or non-normal. Without it, inference would need a custom method for every distribution shape you might encounter, which isn't practical.

Q: Does correlation imply causation? Give an example.

No — a correlation only shows two variables move together, not why. The classic example is ice cream sales and drowning deaths rising together every summer; the real driver is hot weather, which independently increases both ice cream purchases and swimming. Before claiming causation I look for a plausible confounder, check the time order of events, and ideally look for or run a randomized experiment, since randomization is what actually breaks the link to confounders.

Q: When would you use a t-test vs chi-square vs ANOVA?

A t-test compares the means of one or two groups on a numerical variable — one-sample against a fixed target, two-sample across groups, or paired for repeated measures on the same subjects. Chi-square tests whether two categorical variables are associated by comparing observed and expected counts in a contingency table. ANOVA extends the t-test idea to three or more groups' means at once, avoiding the inflated false-positive rate you'd get from running many pairwise t-tests separately.

Q: What is the normal distribution used for, and why does it appear so often?

It models continuous data that clusters symmetrically around a center, like measurement error or heights, and it underlies most classical inference methods because of the Central Limit Theorem. It shows up often because many real quantities are the sum or average of many small independent effects, and the CLT says that kind of sum tends toward a normal shape regardless of what the individual effects look like. In practice I use it for z-scores, confidence intervals, and as the default assumption behind t-tests.

Q: What is Simpson's paradox? Give an example.

It's when a trend present in every subgroup of the data reverses or disappears once the subgroups are combined, usually because a lurking variable is unevenly distributed across the groups. A classic case is two hospitals where Hospital A has a lower survival rate than Hospital B within both mild and severe cases, yet a higher overall survival rate, because Hospital A treats far fewer severe cases. The fix is to always check for a hidden grouping variable before trusting an aggregated statistic.

Q: What is selection bias? How do you detect or avoid it?

Selection bias happens when the way data is collected systematically favors certain outcomes, so the sample no longer represents the population you actually care about — like surveying only engaged app users about "overall satisfaction." I detect it by asking who had a chance to be included in the data and who didn't, and I avoid it by using random or stratified sampling and by being explicit about which population a dataset actually represents before generalizing from it.

Q: What is survivorship bias?

It's analyzing only the entities that "survived" some filtering process, while the ones that didn't survive are invisible and therefore excluded from the analysis. The famous example is WWII engineers wanting to armor the parts of returning planes with the most bullet holes, when those planes survived despite those hits — the real risk was in the spots with no holes, because planes hit there never made it back. In business data this shows up constantly, like analyzing only customers who didn't churn to figure out "what makes a good customer."

Q: What are common pitfalls in A/B testing?

The biggest one is peeking at results daily and stopping the moment p drops below 0.05, which inflates the real false-positive rate well above the nominal 5% because you're effectively running many tests. A close second is testing many metrics or variants and reporting whichever one looks significant, which runs into the same multiple-comparisons problem. The fix for both is deciding the sample size, primary metric, and stopping rule before the test starts, and using a proper power analysis to size the test for the effect you actually care about detecting.

Q: What's the difference between Bayesian and frequentist statistics?

Frequentist statistics treats the population parameter as a fixed constant and makes probability statements about the long-run behavior of a procedure, like a p-value or confidence interval. Bayesian statistics treats the parameter itself as uncertain, starts from a prior belief, and updates it with the data's likelihood into a posterior distribution, which allows direct probability statements about the parameter or hypothesis. I reach for Bayesian methods when data is scarce and a sensible prior exists, or when a stakeholder needs a direct probability statement; otherwise the two approaches often converge to similar answers with enough data.

Q: What's the difference between standard deviation and standard error?

Standard deviation measures the spread of individual data points around the mean of a single sample or population. Standard error measures the spread of a sample statistic, like the sample mean, across many hypothetical repeated samples, and it equals the standard deviation divided by the square root of n. Standard deviation doesn't shrink as you collect more data — the data is just as spread out — but standard error does shrink, because a larger sample gives a more precise estimate of the mean.

Q: How do you detect and handle outliers?

I detect them visually with box plots and histograms, and numerically with the 1.5×IQR rule or z-scores beyond about 3 standard deviations for roughly normal data. Handling them depends on cause: a data-entry error should be corrected or removed, a genuine extreme value (a very large purchase, a true outage) usually shouldn't be deleted but may call for a robust statistic like the median or a transformation like a log scale. I always document what I did with outliers, since silently dropping them can quietly bias the results.

Q: What is statistical power, and why does it matter?

Power is the probability a test correctly detects a real effect when one actually exists — it's 1 minus the Type II error rate. It matters because an underpowered test that finds "no significant difference" is genuinely ambiguous: it could mean there's no effect, or it could just mean the test didn't have enough data to detect the effect that's actually there. I compute power, or the required sample size for a target power, before running a test, not after.

Q: What does skewness tell you about a distribution, and how do you address it?

Skewness tells you which direction the distribution's tail stretches — right-skewed means a few unusually large values pull the mean above the median, as with income, and left-skewed is the mirror image. I address it by choosing robust statistics, like the median and IQR instead of the mean and standard deviation, and in modeling contexts by applying a transformation, like a log transform, that pulls in the long tail and makes the data better behaved for methods that assume normality.

Q: What is R-squared, and what are its limitations?

R-squared is the proportion of variance in the outcome variable explained by the model, ranging from 0 to 1. Its main limitation is that a high R-squared doesn't guarantee the model's assumptions hold — residual patterns like non-constant variance can still invalidate the model's confidence intervals and p-values — and R-squared mechanically increases as you add more predictors even if they're not meaningfully useful, which is why adjusted R-squared or out-of-sample validation is usually the better comparison tool.

Q: What is the law of large numbers, and how does it differ from the Central Limit Theorem?

The law of large numbers says that as sample size grows, the sample mean converges to the true population mean — it's about where the average ends up. The Central Limit Theorem says more than that: it describes the shape of the sampling distribution of the mean around that true value, showing it becomes approximately normal as sample size grows. In short, the law of large numbers tells you the estimate gets accurate, and the CLT tells you how the error around that estimate is distributed, which is what makes confidence intervals and hypothesis tests possible.

🎤 One-Line Interview AnswerI treat every interview statistics question the same way: give the precise definition first, then a concrete example or mechanism, then the practical caveat — because in real analysis the caveat is usually where the actual judgment call lives.

If time is limited, prioritize stages 1 through 4 before anything else — they cover roughly 80% of what shows up in both interviews and daily data science work. Stage 5 (experimentation and pitfalls) is where the highest-leverage mistakes get made in real companies, so it deserves attention before it's urgently needed, not after a bad experiment ships a false conclusion. Stage 6 (Bayesian reasoning) is valuable but can wait until the frequentist foundation is solid, since most Bayesian intuition is easier to build on top of a working understanding of probability and hypothesis testing rather than instead of it.
