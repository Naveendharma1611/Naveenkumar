Module 1 — Number Sense & Arithmetic

Factors · Multiples · Divisibility · Remainders

▼

Factors, Multiples, and Primes

A factor divides a number evenly; a multiple is the result of multiplying by an integer. A prime has exactly two positive factors, 1 and itself, so 1 is not prime. Use prime factorization to find the greatest common factor and least common multiple.

TaskMethod
GCF/HCFMultiply common prime factors using the smaller exponent
LCMMultiply each prime factor using the larger exponent
Divisibility by 3 or 9Digit sum is divisible by 3 or 9
Divisibility by 5Last digit is 0 or 5

Remainders and Estimation

When a problem asks for a remainder, reduce intermediate values modulo the divisor where valid. Estimate before calculating to catch arithmetic slips and implausible answer choices.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. MCQ: Which number is prime? A. 21 B. 29 C. 39 D. 51 Answer: B, 29. Why: 29 has no divisors other than 1 and itself.
  2. Numeric: Find the HCF of 36 and 48. Answer: 12. Why: 12 is the greatest integer dividing both numbers.

Module 2 — Fractions, Ratios & Proportions

Equivalent ratios · Sharing · Direct and inverse variation

▼

Ratios and Proportions

A ratio compares quantities in the same units. Simplify by dividing both terms by their common factor. In a proportion, equivalent ratios are equal; cross multiplication can solve for an unknown when denominators are nonzero.

Sharing in a Ratio

For a total divided in ratio a:b, there are a+b equal parts. One share is the total divided by a+b, then multiplied by the relevant ratio term.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Divide 64 in the ratio 3:5. What is the smaller share? Answer: 24. Why: There are 8 parts; each is 8, so the smaller share is 3 × 8.
  2. MCQ: If 4 notebooks cost 12 dollars at a constant unit price, what do 7 notebooks cost? A. 18 B. 20 C. 21 D. 24 Answer: C, 21. Why: One notebook costs 3 dollars.

Module 3 — Percentages & Change

Percent of a quantity · Percentage points · Successive change

▼

Percentage Basics

x% means x/100. Percentage change is (new − original) / original × 100%. A change from 20% to 25% is an increase of 5 percentage points, or a 25% relative increase from the original rate.

Successive Changes

Apply each percentage change to the value produced by the previous change. A rise of r% followed by a fall of r% does not return to the original, because the second percentage uses a different base.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Find 20% of 350. Answer: 70. Why: 0.20 × 350 = 70.
  2. MCQ: A price rises 20% and then falls 20%. What is the net change? A. No change B. 2% decrease C. 4% decrease D. 4% increase Answer: C, 4% decrease. Why: 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96 of the original.

Module 4 — Averages & Mixtures

Mean · Weighted average · Replacement · Concentration

▼

Means and Weighted Averages

The arithmetic mean is total divided by count. For groups of different sizes, calculate a weighted mean using each group's count; averaging the group means directly is only correct when group sizes are equal.

Mixtures

Track the amount of the relevant ingredient separately from the total mixture. A concentration is ingredient amount divided by total amount; when combining mixtures, add ingredient amounts and total amounts before dividing.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: What is the mean of 12, 16, and 20? Answer: 16. Why: Their sum is 48; divide by 3.
  2. MCQ: A class of 10 averages 70 and a class of 30 averages 80. What is the combined average? A. 75 B. 77.5 C. 78 D. 80 Answer: B, 77.5. Why: (10×70 + 30×80)/40 = 77.5.

Module 5 — Profit, Loss & Discounts

Cost price · Selling price · Markup · Successive discounts

▼

Profit and Loss

Profit is selling price minus cost price; loss is cost price minus selling price. Profit or loss percentage is measured against cost price unless the question specifies another base. A discount percentage is normally measured against marked price.

Discounts

For successive discounts a% and b%, multiply the remaining fractions: (1−a/100)(1−b/100). Do not simply add the rates unless the problem defines a different calculation.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: An item costs 400 and sells for 500. Find the profit percentage. Answer: 25%. Why: Profit is 100; 100/400 × 100% = 25%.
  2. Numeric: A 600 item is discounted by 10%. Find the sale price. Answer: 540. Why: The buyer pays 90% of 600.

Module 6 — Simple & Compound Interest

Principal · Rate · Time · Growth

▼

Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal: SI = P × r × t / 100 when r is an annual percentage and t is in years. Confirm the rate and time units match.

Compound Interest

With annual compounding, amount is A = P(1 + r/100)^t; compound interest is A − P. More frequent compounding changes the formula and result, so follow the convention stated in the question.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Find simple interest on 2,000 at 5% per year for 3 years. Answer: 300. Why: 2000 × 5 × 3 / 100 = 300.
  2. MCQ: 1,000 compounds annually at 10% for 2 years. What is the amount? A. 1,100 B. 1,200 C. 1,210 D. 1,220 Answer: C, 1,210. Why: 1000 × 1.1² = 1210.

Module 7 — Time & Work

Rates · Combined work · Efficiency

▼

Work Rates

If a person completes one job in t time units, their rate is 1/t job per unit time. Add rates for independent workers working together; the time for one job is the reciprocal of the combined rate.

Efficiency

If A is twice as efficient as B, A's work rate is twice B's for the same task. Keep the relationship in rates, not days: time and rate are reciprocals for a fixed workload.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: A completes a task in 6 days and B in 3 days. How long together? Answer: 2 days. Why: Their rates add to 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2 job per day.
  2. MCQ: A worker completes 1/5 of a job per day. How many days for the full job at that rate? A. 4 B. 5 C. 10 D. 20 Answer: B, 5. Why: The reciprocal of 1/5 job per day is 5 days per job.

Module 8 — Speed, Distance & Time

Units · Relative speed · Average speed

▼

Core Relationship

Distance equals speed times time. Convert units before combining values: 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h. Relative speed is the sum of speeds when objects move toward one another and the absolute difference when they move in the same direction.

Average Speed

Average speed is total distance divided by total time, not necessarily the arithmetic mean of speeds. For equal distances at speeds u and v, average speed is 2uv/(u+v).

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Travel 60 km/h for 2.5 hours. How far? Answer: 150 km. Why: 60 × 2.5 = 150.
  2. MCQ: Convert 72 km/h to m/s. A. 18 B. 20 C. 24 D. 25 Answer: B, 20 m/s. Why: Divide by 3.6.

Module 9 — Algebra & Equations

Linear equations · Identities · Substitution

▼

Solve Equations Systematically

Apply the same operation to both sides and simplify one step at a time. For a linear equation ax+b=c, if a is nonzero then x=(c−b)/a. Substitute the solution back to check it.

Translate Words to Algebra

Define variables before writing equations. “Five more than twice x” is 2x+5; “twice the quantity x plus five” is 2(x+5). Parentheses capture the intended grouping.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Solve 2x + 7 = 23. Answer: x = 8. Why: Subtract 7, then divide by 2.
  2. MCQ: If x+y=12 and x−y=4, what is x? A. 4 B. 6 C. 8 D. 10 Answer: C, 8. Why: Add equations to get 2x=16.

Module 10 — Counting & Probability

Product rule · Permutations · Combinations · Events

▼

Counting

Use the product rule for sequential choices when each stage has a known number of options. Permutations count ordered selections; combinations count unordered selections. Check whether repetition is allowed and whether order matters.

CountFormula
Order r items from n distinctn!/(n−r)!
Choose r items from n distinctn!/(r!(n−r)!)
Probability of event Afavorable outcomes / total equally likely outcomes
ComplementP(not A) = 1 − P(A)

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: How many ways to choose 2 people from 5? Answer: 10. Why: Order does not matter: 5×4/2.
  2. MCQ: A bag has 3 red and 2 blue balls. Probability of red on one draw? A. 2/5 B. 3/5 C. 1/2 D. 3/2 Answer: B, 3/5. Why: Three of five equally likely balls are red.

Module 11 — Data Interpretation

Tables · Charts · Percent change · Ratios

▼

Read Before Calculating

Read the title, units, time period, population, and footnotes first. Distinguish counts from percentages and totals from averages. Check whether a chart's axis starts at zero and whether categories or time intervals are comparable.

Compare Carefully

Percentage change uses the original value as its denominator. A difference of 10 units is not the same as a 10% difference; calculate the requested statistic rather than relying on visual impression.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: A table shows sales of 40, 60, and 100. What is their total? Answer: 200. Why: Add the three values.
  2. MCQ: A value increases from 80 to 100. What is its percentage increase? A. 20% B. 25% C. 80% D. 125% Answer: B, 25%. Why: Increase 20 divided by original 80.

Module 12 — Number & Letter Patterns

Differences · Alternation · Rule checking

▼

Find a Consistent Rule

Check first differences, second differences, ratios, alternating subsequences, squares, cubes, and position-dependent rules. A pattern should explain every given term; avoid choosing a rule that only fits the last pair.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Find the next term: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, __. Answer: 36. Why: These are squares of 1 through 6.
  2. MCQ: Find the next term: 3, 6, 12, 24, __. A. 30 B. 36 C. 48 D. 60 Answer: C, 48. Why: Each term doubles.

Module 13 — Syllogisms & Conclusions

Quantifiers · Necessary conclusions · Negation

▼

Reason Only from Given Premises

Treat statements as premises, even when they conflict with real-world knowledge. “All A are B” does not mean all B are A. “Some” means at least one; it does not imply “most.” Test whether a conclusion must follow, not whether it might be true.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. MCQ: All roses are flowers. Some flowers are red. Must some roses be red? A. Yes B. No, it does not follow C. All roses are red D. No roses are flowers Answer: B. Why: The red flowers need not be roses.
  2. MCQ: No cats are reptiles. All tigers are cats. What follows? A. Some reptiles are tigers B. No tigers are reptiles C. All reptiles are cats D. No conclusion Answer: B. Why: Tigers are within cats, and cats are disjoint from reptiles.

Module 14 — Arrangements & Constraints

Linear order · Relative positions · Cases

▼

Translate Clues into Constraints

For seating and ordering puzzles, draw positions and convert clues into relations such as “A is immediately left of B” or “C is not at an endpoint.” Place fixed constraints first, branch on unresolved choices, and reject a case as soon as it contradicts a clue.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: How many linear arrangements are possible for 4 distinct people? Answer: 24. Why: 4! = 24.
  2. MCQ: A sits left of B and B left of C. Which must be true? A. C is left of A B. A is left of C C. B is left of A D. A is adjacent to C Answer: B. Why: Order is transitive; adjacency is not implied.

Module 15 — Directions & Relationships

Relative direction · Family relations · Diagrams

▼

Directions

Fix a north-east-south-west coordinate frame and track each move from the current position. For shortest distance, combine net horizontal and vertical displacement; do not confuse path length with straight-line distance.

Family Relationships

Draw a small family tree and mark gender or generation only when stated. Read “A is the brother of B's mother” from the named person outward rather than guessing from the sentence's final noun.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. Numeric: Walk 3 km east, then 4 km north. What is the straight-line distance from the start? Answer: 5 km. Why: A 3-4-5 right triangle.
  2. MCQ: A is B's mother's brother. What is A to B? A. Paternal uncle B. Maternal uncle C. Cousin D. Grandfather Answer: B, maternal uncle. Why: A is the brother of B's mother.

Module 16 — Grammar & Sentence Correction

Agreement · Tense · Pronouns · Parallel structure

▼

Core Editing Checks

Check subject-verb agreement, consistent tense, pronoun reference, modifiers, articles, and parallel structure. In long sentences, identify the grammatical subject rather than matching the verb to the nearest noun.

IssueCheck
AgreementSingular subject takes a singular verb in the present tense
TenseKeep time relationships consistent and intentional
PronounMake the antecedent clear and agreement unambiguous
ModifierPlace descriptive phrases next to the words they modify
ParallelismUse the same grammatical form in a list or comparison

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. MCQ: Choose the correct sentence. A. Each of the reports are ready. B. Each of the reports is ready. C. Each reports is ready. D. Each of reports are ready. Answer: B. Why: “Each” is singular and takes “is.”
  2. MCQ: Choose the parallel sentence. A. She likes reading, to swim, and cycling. B. She likes reading, swimming, and cycling. C. She likes to read, swimming, and to cycle. D. She likes read, swim, and cycling. Answer: B. Why: The list uses matching gerund forms.

Module 17 — Vocabulary, Comprehension & Arguments

Context · Main idea · Inference · Assumptions

▼

Vocabulary in Context

Use surrounding sentences to infer an unfamiliar word's role and tone. Replace it mentally with a candidate meaning and check that the sentence still makes sense; don't select a familiar synonym that contradicts context.

Reading and Critical Reasoning

Separate the author's claim, evidence, assumptions, and conclusion. An inference must be supported by the passage, while an assumption is an unstated idea needed for an argument to work. For strengthen/weaken questions, target the reasoning link, not just a related fact.

🏋️ Practice Questions

  1. MCQ: A passage says a pilot program improved results in two schools. Which conclusion is safest? A. It will work in every school B. It is promising in the tested settings but needs broader evidence C. It caused every improvement D. It should replace all existing programs Answer: B. Why: The evidence supports a limited conclusion, not universal generalization or causation.
  2. MCQ: An argument says a new bus route reduced commute times because average times fell afterward. Which evidence most directly strengthens a causal claim? A. A comparable control route had no similar change B. The bus is painted blue C. More people own bicycles D. The route map is longer Answer: A. Why: A comparable control helps distinguish the route's effect from broader changes.

Reasoning & Verbal Checklist

  • Number patterns: differences, ratios, alternating rules, squares, cubes.
  • Syllogisms: infer only what must follow from the stated premises.
  • Arrangements: diagram positions and apply constraints one at a time.
  • Directions: track net horizontal and vertical displacement separately.
  • Grammar: check agreement, tense, reference, modifiers, and parallelism.
  • Comprehension: distinguish passage facts, supported inference, and outside assumptions.
  • Timed test: estimate first, skip-and-return when stuck, and reserve time to check units.
Read the exact question and units before calculating. For logic and verbal items, do not import unstated assumptions; for numerical items, verify the answer against the original quantities.
